\begin{abstract}
Image classification is a major application of deep learning because models can learn hierarchical visual representations from data. This study compares a simple convolutional neural network (CNN) baseline, a CNN with dropout, and a pretrained ResNet-18 using transfer learning on CIFAR-10. The dataset contains 60,000 RGB images of size 32$\times$32 pixels distributed across 10 classes. The official 50,000 training images were split into 45,000 training and 5,000 validation samples, while the 10,000 official test images were preserved for final evaluation. Three experiments were conducted with Adam: E1 CNN baseline with learning rate 0.001, E2 CNN with dropout 0.5 and learning rate 0.001, and E3 pretrained ResNet-18 with learning rate 0.0001. Training used batch size 128, a maximum of 20 epochs, seed 42, and early stopping based on validation loss with patience 5. Evaluation used loss, accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, training curves, and confusion matrices. Test results show that E1 achieved 68.92\% accuracy and 68.53\% F1, E2 achieved 67.39\% and 67.22\%, while E3 reached 95.22\% for both accuracy and F1. Training analysis indicates that E1 and E2 remained relatively stable without severe overfitting, whereas E3 developed a larger train-validation gap after approximately epoch 7 and stopped early at epoch 12. Error analysis shows that E1 and E2 produced more confusion among visually similar animal classes, while E3 produced a much stronger diagonal pattern. Overall, transfer learning with ResNet-18 provided substantially more effective representations than the simple CNN configurations used in this study.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Image classification is an important computer vision problem and a major application area of deep learning. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) use convolution operations to learn local patterns and hierarchical representations, making them effective for visual recognition. However, a simple CNN may have limited representation capacity when the visual variability of the data is high.

One common regularization strategy is dropout, which randomly deactivates units during training and can reduce co-adaptation and overfitting [1]. Another strategy is transfer learning, where representations learned from a source domain are adapted to a target task [2]. ResNet introduced residual learning to simplify the optimization of deeper networks and has become a strong backbone for image recognition [3].

This study uses CIFAR-10 because it provides ten balanced classes of small RGB images [4]. The study compares three configurations: a CNN baseline, the same CNN with dropout 0.5, and pretrained ResNet-18 with transfer learning. The research questions are: (1) does dropout improve generalization for the simple CNN, (2) how large is the benefit of transfer learning over the baseline, and (3) which configuration provides the best balance between test performance and training behavior?

The contribution is a reproducible comparison based on real training runs, including quantitative evaluation, training analysis, and error analysis.

\section{Related Work}
Dropout is a widely used neural-network regularization method. Srivastava \textit{et al.} showed that randomly dropping units during training can reduce co-adaptation and improve generalization [1]. In this work, dropout is explicitly evaluated on the baseline CNN.

Transfer learning has been studied as a mechanism for transferring knowledge from a source domain to a target task [2]. Pan and Yang provide a broad analysis of transfer learning and its application across different machine-learning settings [2]. Here, pretrained ImageNet features are adapted to CIFAR-10 using ResNet-18.

Residual learning was introduced by He \textit{et al.}, allowing deeper networks to be optimized more effectively through residual connections [3]. The resulting ResNet family has become a standard architecture for visual representation learning.

CIFAR-10 is a standard benchmark dataset for image classification containing 60,000 32$\times$32 color images across ten classes [4]. Adam is an adaptive optimization method based on estimates of first and second moments of gradients [5] and is used for all experiments in this study.

\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Dataset}
CIFAR-10 contains 60,000 RGB images of size 32$\times$32 pixels distributed across 10 classes: airplane, automobile, bird, cat, deer, dog, frog, horse, ship, and truck. The official dataset contains 50,000 training images and 10,000 test images; each class contains 5,000 training and 1,000 test samples [4].

The implementation randomly split the 50,000 official training images into 45,000 training and 5,000 validation samples using seed 42. The 10,000 official test images remained isolated for final testing. Therefore, test data were not used during model training or model selection.

\subsection{Preprocessing}
For E1 and E2, images were kept at 32$\times$32 pixels. Training used random horizontal flip and random crop with padding 4, while validation and test data used no augmentation. CIFAR-10 mean $(0.4914, 0.4822, 0.4465)$ and standard deviation $(0.2470, 0.2435, 0.2616)$ were used for normalization.

For E3, images were resized to 224$\times$224 pixels to match the pretrained ResNet-18 input. Training used random horizontal flip and random crop with padding 8, followed by ImageNet normalization with mean $(0.485, 0.456, 0.406)$ and standard deviation $(0.229, 0.224, 0.225)$. Validation and test data used resizing and normalization without augmentation.

\subsection{Baseline CNN}
The baseline CNN contains three convolutional blocks with 32, 64, and 128 channels. ReLU activation follows each convolution, max pooling is used in the first two blocks, and adaptive average pooling is followed by a linear classifier for ten classes. E1 uses dropout 0.0 and therefore represents the plain baseline.

\subsection{ResNet-18 Transfer Learning}
The main model is ResNet-18 pretrained on ImageNet. Its final classification layer was replaced by a linear layer with ten outputs for CIFAR-10. All parameters were fine-tuned on the target dataset. ResNet-18 was selected because residual learning supports optimization of deeper networks [3], while transfer learning provides a strong pretrained representation [2].

\subsection{Loss and Optimization}
All experiments use cross-entropy loss through PyTorch \texttt{CrossEntropyLoss}. The loss is defined as
\begin{equation}
L=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log p(y_i\mid x_i),
\end{equation}
where $p(y_i\mid x_i)$ denotes the predicted probability of the correct class. Optimization uses Adam [5]. No learning-rate scheduler or weight decay was used. Dropout in E2 is the only explicit regularization change between E1 and E2.

\subsection{Training Analysis}
E1 improved from 30.70\% training accuracy at epoch 1 to 69.26\% at epoch 20, while validation accuracy reached 68.50\%. The small late-stage gap suggests no severe overfitting, although the final performance indicates limited representational capacity.

E2 improved from 26.81\% to 62.82\% training accuracy and reached 66.26\% validation accuracy at epoch 20. The validation accuracy was 66.36\% at epoch 19. Dropout therefore did not produce a test-performance gain over the baseline.

E3 reached 95.74\% validation accuracy at epoch 7 while training accuracy was already 98.92\%. After this point, training accuracy remained close to 99\% while validation accuracy fluctuated and validation loss rose. This pattern indicates mild overfitting after approximately epoch 7. Early stopping terminated training at epoch 12.

\subsection{Error Analysis}
The confusion matrices for E1 and E2 show more off-diagonal mass among animal classes. The most visible confusions involve bird, cat, dog, deer, and frog, which can share similar shapes, textures, and color patterns at 32$\times$32 resolution. E2 preserves a similar error pattern, suggesting that dropout did not substantially change the main source of classification errors.

In contrast, E3 exhibits a much stronger diagonal pattern with fewer off-diagonal errors. Residual mistakes are still visible mainly among visually similar animal classes, particularly cat and dog. The confusion matrices are consistent with the much higher test accuracy and F1-score of E3.

\subsection{Discussion}
The difference between E1 and E2 suggests that changing regularization does not automatically overcome the feature-extraction limitations of a simple CNN. Dropout is intended to reduce co-adaptation [1], but for this configuration the model without dropout still obtained slightly better test accuracy and F1-score. E3, in contrast, benefits from pretrained representations and residual learning [2], [3].

From a generalization perspective, E1 and E2 maintain relatively close training and validation curves, whereas E3 develops a larger gap after validation performance peaks. The stronger representation capacity of E3 yields a substantial test-performance gain but also makes overfitting appear earlier. For this reason, early stopping is useful for the transfer-learning configuration.

\section{Conclusion}
This study compared a CNN baseline, CNN with dropout 0.5, and pretrained ResNet-18 transfer learning for CIFAR-10 image classification. The final test results show that E3 achieved the best performance with 95.22\% accuracy and 95.22\% F1-score, outperforming E1 by 26.30 percentage points and E2 by 27.83 points. E2 did not improve over E1 under the tested dropout setting. Training analysis indicates that E1 and E2 remained comparatively stable, while E3 developed mild overfitting after its best validation epoch and was stopped early.

Practically, the results indicate that transfer learning with ResNet-18 is highly effective for CIFAR-10 classification when a strong pretrained representation is available, although it has higher computational cost and requires careful monitoring of overfitting. Future work can evaluate other pretrained backbones, tune augmentation and regularization more systematically, and perform more detailed per-class error analysis.
